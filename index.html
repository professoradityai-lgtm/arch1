Born in the Greek port city of Syracuse around 287 BC, Archimedes was the son of Phidias, an astronomer, and a relative of King Hieron II. He likely studied in Alexandria alongside the successors of Euclid — then returned home to think, alone, for the rest of his life.

Where others debated, Archimedes proved. He fused pure geometry with physical law, inventing the rigorous method of exhaustion — an ancestor of integral calculus, eighteen centuries before Newton and Leibniz.

He wrote in letters to fellow mathematicians, drawing figures in the sand, in ashes, on his own skin in oil. Plutarch tells us he could forget to eat, carried by force to the bath — where he would draw diagrams in the steam.

His machines defended a city. His theorems built the modern world. This is the scroll of his mind — descend.
